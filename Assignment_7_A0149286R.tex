\section{Nash Welfare}
Suppose not, that there is an allocation $A^*$ that maximises
Nash welfare, and that there exists two players $i,j\in N$ such that
the bundle of $j$ is not empty and 
\begin{equation}
    \forall g\in A_j^* : v_i(A_i^*) < v_i(A_j^*\backslash\{g\})
\end{equation}
Since $NW(A)>0$, we have $\forall A_i^*: v_i(A_i^*)>0$.
Consider $g^*\in A_j^*$ that minimizes the ratio $\frac{v_j(g^*)}{v_i(g^*)}$.
\[\forall g\in A_j^* : 
\frac{v_j(g^*)}{v_i(g^*)} \leq \frac{v_j(g)}{v_i(g)}\]
\[\forall g\in A_j^* : v_j(g^*)v_i(g) \leq v_i(g^*)v_j(g)\]
By summing over all $g\in A_j^*$,
\begin{equation}
    v_j(g^*)v_i(A_j^*) \leq v_i(g^*)v_j(A_j^*)
\end{equation}
Using $(1)$,
\begin{equation}
    v_i(g^*) + v_i(A_i^*) < v_i(g^*) + v_i(A_j^*\backslash\{g^*\})  
    = v_i(A_j^*)
\end{equation}
Combining $(2),(3)$,
\begin{equation}
    v_j(g^*)(v_i(g^*) + v_i(A_i^*))
    <v_j(g^*)v_i(A_j^*)\leq v_i(g^*)v_j(A_j^*)
\end{equation}
Finally,
\begin{equation}
    v_i(g^*)v_j(A_j^*) - v_j(g^*)(v_i(g^*) + v_i(A_i^*)) > 0
\end{equation}
The value of the Nash welfare is
\[NW(A*)=v_i(A_i^*)v_j(A_j^*)\prod_{k\in N\backslash\{i,j\}}v_k(A_k^*)\]
Moving $g^*$ from $j$'s bundle to $i$'s bundle, the new allocation
$A^{**}$ has Nash welfare
\[NW(A^{**})=(v_i(A_i^*)+v_i(g^*))(v_j(A_j^*)-v_j(g^*))
\prod_{k\in N\backslash\{i,j\}}v_k(A_k^*)\]
Notice that
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    &(v_i(A_i^*)+v_i(g^*))(v_j(A_j^*)-v_j(g^*))\\
    &=v_i(A_i^*)v_j(A_j^*)+v_i(g^*)v_j(A_j^*)
    -v_j(g^*)(v_i(A_i^*)+v_i(g^*))\\
    &> v_i(A_i^*)v_j(A_j^*) \quad\text{(from (5))}\\
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Then
\[NW(A^{**})>v_i(A_i^*)v_j(A_j^*)\prod_{k\in N\backslash\{i,j\}}v_k(A_k^*)
=NW(A^*)\]
A contradiction as $NW(A^*)$ maximizes the Nash welfare.
Thus, an allocation $A^*$ that maximizes Nash welfare is $EF1$.

\section{Three Players, Four Goods}

\subsection{Utilitarian Welfare}
Assign $g_1$ to Player 1, $g_2,g_3,g_4$ to Player 3,
to get the maximum \textit{utilitarian welfare} of
\[30+6+12+12=60\]
It is not $EF1$ as 
\[v_2(\emptyset)=0<v_2(g_i,g_j),\forall i,j\in \{2,3,4\}\]
Player 2 envies Player 3 for over 1 good.

\subsection{Egalitarian Welfare}
Assign $g_1$ to Player 1, $g_2$ to Player 3, $g_3,g_4$ to Player 2,
to get maximum \textit{egalitarian welfare} of
\[\min \{30,6,5+5\}=6\]
It is not $EF1$ as
\[v_3(g_2)=6<v_3(g_3)=v_3(g_4)=12\]
Player 3 envies Player 2 for over 1 good.

\subsection{Nash Welfare}
Assign $g_1$ to Player 1, $g_4$ to Player 3, $g_2,g_3$ to Player 2,
to get maximum \textit{Nash welfare} of
\[30\times 5\times 18=2700\]
It is $EF1$.

\section{Strong $EF1$}

\subsection{Round-Robin}
For every player $j,g_j\in A_j$ can be the first pick for $j$.
If $i$ is ahead of $j$ in the order, then clearly $i$ does not envy
$j$ in every pick and 
\[v_i(A_i)\geq v_i(A_j) \geq v_i(A_j\backslash\{g_j\})\]
If $i$ is behind $j$ in the order, then we consider the first round
to start at $i$'s pick, ignoring $j$'s first pick of $g_j$,
and $i$ does not envy $j$ for the rest of the picks.
\[v_i(A_i)\geq v_i(A_j\backslash\{g_j\})\]
The output of the Round-Robin algorithm is s-EF1.

\subsection{Envy-Cycle Elimination}
We can think of the items each player has as being bundles,
and each time an item is allocated, it is allocated to the bundle.
The algorithm either moves bundles to different players, 
or allocates new items to bundles.
For each player $j$'s bundle $A_j$ in the output of the algorithm,
the last item allocated to the bundle can be $g_j\in A_j$. 
Since allocations to bundles are unenvied before allocating,
each player $i$ will not envy player $j$ for up to good $g_j$.
The output of the Envy-Cycle algorithm is s-EF1.

\section{MMS Allocation}

\subsection{Two Agents}
Two agents can use a "cut-and-choose algorithm".
Player 1 divides the set of goods into two bundles $A_1,A_2$ 
as equally valued as possible in their view, 
guaranteeing that one bundle is valued
at $v_1(A_1)=MMS_1$, and the other is $v_1(A_2)=v_1(G)-MMS_1\geq MMS_1$.
When Player 2 picks the bundle for themselves, they can just pick
the weakly higher-valued one for themselves. 
Since $MMS_2\leq \frac{v_2(G)}{2}$ and $v_2(A_1)+v_2(A_2)=v_2(G)$,
we must have at either $v_2(A_1) \geq MMS_2$ or $v_2(A_2) \geq MMS_2$.
Then both players have their $MMS$ share and we have an MMS allocation.

\subsection{At Least Three Agents}
We prove the contrapositive.
Suppose there does not exist a Pareto-optimal MMS allocation.
Then for any MMS allocation $A$, there is another MMS allocation $A^*$
for which there exists a player $j$ 
that can improve the value of their bundle,
with no other player reducing their value.
Then, the total valuation of the players' bundles of goods increases.
\[\sum_i v_i(A_i) < \sum_i v_i(A_i^*)\]
There is no upper bound to the total valuation of the goods.
But the total valuation cannot exceed
\[\sum_i v_i(G)\]
This is a contradiction, thus an MMS allocation cannot exist.
Thus, if there exists an MMS allocation in a certain instance,
there also exists a Pareto-optimal MMS allocation in that instance.
